Heard Too Late

A short story about a family and sentencing in the District Court.

This Tongan family is fictional, but their story draws on New Zealand law and Pasifika-led research. No single story represents all Pasifika peoples.

2.14amNana Alisimobile

Ōtara, 2.14am

You're working a late-night shift when your grandmother rings. Nana Alisi never rings this late; you nearly let it go to voicemail.

"They've arrested Sione. He's at the police station asking for you."

Sione is your cousin, 19, and the one the little kids follow around. Since his dad died and mum moved to Brisbane for work he's been on Alisi's couch (or in his car, when the house is full). Last month the packhouse cut his shifts.

Tonight he drove two older guys to a liquor store and waited outside while they broke in. When they ran, one of them knocked a security guard to the ground.

What do you do?

They won't let you see him. An officer says he'll appear before a judge in the morning.

Uncle Tevita replies "get him a lawyer". Nobody replies to that.

The waiting area is packed. Around ten, a finds you. She has about five minutes.

What do you tell her?

Alisi's house already has eleven people in three bedrooms. The lawyer doubts a judge will accept it as a bail address.

She writes that down. "Okay. Then I don't have a suitable address to give the judge."

You start telling her about his mum going to Brisbane, and his nights in the car. She listens, then says, "That may matter later. Right now I need a suitable address."

Without a suitable address or a plan to manage the risks, Sione is .

Kept in prison while the case carries on, before any sentence.

He spends the next five months there. The packhouse fires him in the second week, and his car gets towed before anyone thinks to move it.

Nobody in court had time to explore why he'd been sleeping in his car.

The law behind this

Under s 8 of the Bail Act 2000 the judge weighs risk: whether someone will turn up to court, interfere with witnesses or reoffend. Remand time comes off any later prison sentence, though not automatically off home detention (Parole Act 2002, ss 90–91).

Skip ahead four months

Four months later

Being heard

Sione pleads guilty. His lawyer mentions "section 27" on the phone; you have to ask what that is.

Section 27 of the Sentencing Act 2002

Before someone is sentenced, they can ask the court to hear from people who know them: about their personal, family, whānau, community and cultural background, how it might connect to the offending, and what support is around them. The judge has to listen unless there's a special reason that makes it unnecessary or inappropriate, and can to arrange it.

Pause the case until a later date.

Does that guarantee a lower sentence? No. The judge decides how much weight to give it.

Sentencing is in three weeks. Try each option.

Since March 2024, has not paid for section 27 reports, so the family would have to pay for one. Alisi says she'll ask around the family for help.

Government funding for a lawyer, and some costs, for people who can't afford one.

You swap a shift to get the day off, then the hearing gets moved. You swap again, it moves again, and this time your manager says no.

To the Judge,

I'm Sione's older cousin. I want you to know he's a kind, hard-working boy and what he's like at home. When Alisi (our grandmother) was young, police came to her house before it was light, looking for overstayers. Sione walks the little ones to school...

You finish your letter at 4am. The lawyer files it, but explains the court will need to see how Sione's experiences relate to his offending, and a family letter sometimes counts for less than an independent report.

The law and research behind this

Since the Legal Services Amendment Act 2024, legal aid won't pay for section 27 reports or statements. Pacific people who shared their experience for a 2023 Pacific-led report (Report Two) describe shift work and childcare making it hard to come to court.

In Berkland v R (2022), the Supreme Court said background can reduce a sentence where it helped cause the offending, and independent evidence may be more convincing. Barrister Kingi Snelgar says some judges prefer evidence from a report writer (Equal Justice Project, 2025). Legal scholar Khylee Quince worries reports push family voices out (Barbara-Luhia Graham, 2025).

Go to sentencing

Courtroom 3, five months after the arrest

Sentencing

The guard's is read. He describes how that night has affected his sleep. Sione looks at the floor the whole time.

The victim's own account of how the crime affected them.

The judge sets a starting point, then considers the guilty plea, Sione's age and his background.

The two-year line

If the final sentence is two years or less and there's a suitable home address, the judge can consider instead of prison. So a background discount can decide which one is available.

Serving a sentence at an approved home address, with an electronic monitor.

Since June 2025, discounts for personal factors are generally capped at 40%.

Alisi has been trying to find the money for a report.

Alisi found the money

Alisi borrows it from her sister. A report writer who knows the Tongan community in Ōtara meets Alisi and Sione. Her report traces the dawn raids through to the lost shifts and the night in the car, and sets him up to live at Uncle Tevita's in Māngere.

The judge gives a discount for his background. The end sentence is just under two years, so it can become home detention. The judge accounts for the five months already in custody, and sentences Sione to a few months' home detention at Uncle Tevita's.

Alisi couldn't find the money

The judge only has your letter, and accepts Sione is young and pleaded guilty early, but the letter doesn't clearly link his background to the offending, and there's nowhere for him to live.

The end sentence is a bit over two years, so home detention isn't available. Sione goes to prison.

On the judge's desk
  • Your letter

Which ending you got was random. Practically, outcomes depend on each case's facts.

Either way, Sione has already spent five months in custody.

Sources

The two endings adapt Barbara-Luhia Graham's story of Fetu. Home detention is only possible if the prison sentence would otherwise be two years or less (Sentencing Act 2002, s 15A). The 40% discount cap is in s 9Q; s 9R allows more where the cap would be manifestly unjust.

Back to the top of the cliff

What if it went differently?

Should we build a fence at the top of the cliff, or park an ambulance at the bottom? Section 27 is like the ambulance. It turns up after most of the damage is done. Barbara-Luhia Graham records the criticism that it is "too little too late".

Where these ideas come from

If someone you love is facing sentencing

  • Ask their lawyer about section 27. Family and community members can speak in court; a paid report isn't the only way to be heard.
  • For free legal help: your local Community Law Centre, Citizens Advice Bureau (0800 367 222) or, if you're under 25, Youth Law (0800 884 529).
  • Need a bail address? Organisations such as the Grace Foundation Charitable Trust provide bail accommodation and support (gracefoundation.co.nz).
