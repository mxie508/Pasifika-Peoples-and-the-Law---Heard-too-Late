Heard too late

A short story you play, about who gets heard in court, and when.

This family is made up, but every barrier they meet comes from New Zealand law and Pacific-led research. Pacific peoples are many peoples; this is one family's story. Content note: arrest, prison, assault.

Ōtara, 2.14am

You're on a break at the distribution centre when Kui, your grandmother, calls.

"Vili's been arrested. He's at the station. He's asking for you."

Vili is your cousin. He's 19. Since his mum moved to Brisbane for work, he's slept on Kui's couch, or in his car when the house is full. Last month the packhouse cut his shifts.

Tonight he drove two older guys to a liquor store. They broke in. A security guard was hurt when they ran.

Vili did something wrong, and someone was hurt. Both are true. This story is about what the court gets to know about Vili, and when.

What do you do?

They won't let you see him. An officer says he'll go before a judge in the morning.

Uncle Tevita says get him a lawyer. Nobody knows how.

Go to court

Manukau District Court, 9am

Before court

The waiting room is full. Vili is one of dozens on today's list. A calls you over. She has a few minutes.

A free lawyer at court for people who don't have their own lawyer yet.

What do you tell her?

Kui's house already has eleven people in three bedrooms. The lawyer doubts a judge will accept it.

Honest. It also means there's no address to bail him to.

You start on his mum leaving, the lost shifts, the car. She stops you. "That might matter later, at sentencing. Today is about bail."

The judge asks about an address. There isn't a suitable one. Vili is .

Kept in prison while the case carries on, before any sentence.

He spends four months there. He loses his job. His car is towed.

Everything the judge will later need to know about Vili existed this morning. There was nowhere to put it.

Where this comes from

Bail turns on risk, such as whether someone will come back to court or reoffend (Bail Act 2000, s 8). An address helps a court manage that risk. Remand time counts towards any later prison sentence. It doesn't bring back the job.

From the counsel table

In 2025 I interned with the Police Prosecution Service at Manukau. I'm not Pasifika; my parents are Chinese. Prosecutors often handled twenty-plus matters a session, from files received that morning. I saw people held on remand for months, as far as I could tell because they had nowhere to go. I believe I came across a section 27 report once, and it passed me by. My view was from one side of the room, so this story leans on Pacific research rather than on what I saw.

Skip ahead four months

Four months later

Being heard

Vili pleads guilty. His lawyer mentions a law that can bring his background into the courtroom.

Section 27, in plain words

Before sentencing, a person can ask the court to hear from people about their family, community and culture, how that connects to the offending, and what support is around them. The judge must listen unless there's a special reason not to, can to make time for it, and can suggest it if nobody asks.

Pause the case and pick it up on a later date.

Being heard doesn't promise a shorter sentence. The judge decides how much it counts.

Sentencing is in three weeks. Try each option.

Since March 2024, no longer pays for these reports. Your family would have to pay privately.

Government funding that pays for a lawyer, and some costs, for people who can't afford them.

Kui says she'll ask around for the money. She doesn't know yet if she can find it.

The hearing gets moved twice. The third time, your manager won't give you the day off. Legal aid can't cover costs of family speaking either.

You write it at 4am after a shift: Kui's story of police at the door before dawn in 1976, Vili walking the little cousins to school, the first night he slept in the car.

It reaches the judge. But it's from family, not an "independent" writer, and nobody has linked his history to the offending the way the law looks for.

Where this comes from

The Legal Services Amendment Act 2024 stopped legal aid paying for section 27 reports and statements, written or spoken. Report Two's Knowledge Holders describe shift work, childcare and cost as barriers to coming to court.

In Berkland v R, the Supreme Court said background can lower a sentence where it helped cause the offending, and that independent evidence may be more convincing. One barrister has reported judges hesitating to hear cultural evidence without a report writer. Scholars warn this can push family voices aside.

Courtroom 3, six months after the call

Sentencing

The guard's is read. He still doesn't sleep well. His voice matters too, and the law protects it.

The victim's own account of how the crime affected them, given to the judge.

The judge sets a starting point, then adjusts for Vili's guilty plea, age and background.

Why two years matters

If the final sentence is two years or less, the judge can consider instead of prison. A discount for background can be the difference.

Serving a sentence at an approved home address, with an electronic monitor.

Since June 2025, discounts for personal factors in prison sentences are generally capped at 40% in total.

Kui has been trying to find the money for a report.

Kui found the money

A report writer who knows the Tongan community in Ōtara spends hours with Kui and Vili. The report connects the dawn raid, the crowded house and the lost shifts to how Vili ended up in that car. It finds him a place at Uncle Tevita's in Māngere and a Pacific youth programme.

The judge gives a discount for his background. The sentence comes in just under two years: home detention at Uncle Tevita's.

On the judge's desk
  • Your letter
  • An independent report
  • A plan for housing and support

Kui couldn't find the money

There's no report. Your letter is read. The judge accepts that Vili is young and pleaded guilty, but nothing independent links his background to the offending, and there's no plan for where he'd live.

The sentence comes in just over two years. Vili goes to prison.

On the judge's desk
  • Your letter

The offence and the family were the same. The difference was whether there was money that month.

Either way, the remand, the lost job and the guard's injury had already happened. Section 27 can change a sentence. It can't reach back to the night of the call.

Where this comes from

The two endings borrow a device from Barbara-Luhia Graham, whose story of Fetu turns on whether a report is written. Home detention needs a sentence of two years or less (Sentencing Act 2002, s 15A). The 40% cap (s 9Q) has an exception for manifest injustice.

Replay with change

An old saying asks whether to build a fence at the top of a cliff or park an ambulance at the bottom. Section 27 is the ambulance. It helps the people it reaches, but it arrives last.

Here is Vili's story again. Switch on changes Pacific Knowledge Holders and researchers have called for, and watch where his story first gets heard.

Vili's story first reaches the system at sentencing, six months after the call

  1. Before the call

    Nobody outside the family knows Vili is struggling.

    A Pacific youth service already knows Vili. When his shifts are cut, it helps with work and housing. Maybe the phone never rings.

  2. The night of the arrest

    Nobody in the family knows how to get a lawyer.

    A community law worker rings Kui next morning and explains what happens, in the language she prefers.

  3. The bail hearing

    No address. Four months on remand.

    The liaison officer finds Uncle Tevita's address before the case is called. Vili gets bail and keeps his job.

  4. Being heard

    A report depends on money. Speaking depends on time off.

    The report gets written whether or not Kui finds the money.

    A navigator helps you speak in person, and the hearing is set around your shift.

  5. Across the whole cliff

    Nobody can see where Pacific people are lost along the way.

    Agencies can see where Pacific people fall through, and be held to fixing it.

The bottom of the cliff: sentencing

The court hears Vili's story for the first time, after everything else has happened.

Where these changes come from

Report Two's Knowledge Holders; Graham and Khylee Quince (navigators); and the Ministry of Justice, which preferred funding reports by accredited writers. The detail belongs in talanoa with Pacific communities, including people harmed by crime, whose safety comes first.

The cliff in numbers

  • 8.9% and 14.4%Pacific share of the population (2023 Census) and of the prison population (June 2026).
  • 9 to 2,429Legal aid-funded section 27 reports, 2017 to 2022. Since funding stopped, substantially fewer reach the courts.
  • 11% and 7%Report use by Pacific and European/Other offenders for certain sentences in 2021–22. The Ministry warned ending funding would widen that gap.
  • About 10%The Ministry's estimate of a report's average discount, which now competes for room under the 40% cap.

These figures come from different years and don't alone prove discrimination at sentencing.

If someone you love is facing sentencing

  • Ask their lawyer about section 27. Family and community can speak in court; a paid report isn't the only way.
  • The court can pause the case to allow this.
  • For free legal help, contact your local Community Law Centre.

General information, not legal advice. Why a story? Pacific scholars use storytelling to show the law from the inside, and to ask whose knowledge counts.

Being heard matters. Justice shouldn't wait for the bottom of the cliff.
